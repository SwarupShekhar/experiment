In August 1971, psychologist **Philip Zimbardo** converted a basement corridor at Stanford University into a mock prison. Two dozen male college students, screened as psychologically healthy, were paid $15 a day and randomly assigned to be guards or prisoners. The study was planned to last two weeks. It was stopped after six days.

## The story most people know

The prisoners were "arrested" at home by real police, blindfolded, deloused, dressed in smocks and referred to only by number. The guards wore khaki uniforms and mirrored sunglasses and carried batons. Within a day the prisoners rebelled and barricaded a cell; the guards broke the rebellion with a fire extinguisher. Over the following nights, the guards invented humiliating routines: repeated counts through the night, push-ups, cleaning toilets by hand, and making prisoners chant insults about a fellow prisoner.

Several prisoners were released early after acute distress. The study ended when **Christina Maslach**, a recent PhD who had come to help conduct interviews, saw prisoners being marched to the toilet with bags on their heads and objected strongly. Zimbardo later wrote that of the roughly fifty outsiders who saw the prison, she was the only one who questioned its morality.

The takeaway that entered textbooks: put good people in a bad situation, and the situation wins.

## What the popular version leaves out

**The guards were told what was expected.** In an orientation before the study, Zimbardo told the guards they could create fear, frustration and a sense of powerlessness in the prisoners, and that "we" would have all the power. The student who played the warden, David Jaffe, coached guards who seemed too soft. This matters: it turns "guards spontaneously became cruel" into something closer to "guards did what the people running the study signalled they wanted."

**Zimbardo was not a neutral observer.** He played the prison superintendent. By his own later account, he got so absorbed in the role that he stopped seeing the study clearly.

**Some participants say they were acting.** Dave Eshelman, the most notorious guard (nicknamed "John Wayne" by the prisoners), later said he deliberately modelled himself on a sadistic prison captain from the film *Cool Hand Luke*, as a kind of experiment of his own. Douglas Korpi, the prisoner whose breakdown is the study's most famous moment, told a journalist decades later that he was partly faking to get out, although he has given different accounts over the years.

**It was a demonstration, not a controlled experiment.** The findings were written up in 1973, in a criminology journal and a US Navy research report, and then mostly through books, lectures and a documentary. There was no control group and only one run, with fewer than two dozen participants. It's a vivid case study, not a measurement.

In 2018 French researcher **Thibault Le Texier** published an archive-based critique, and a 2019 article in *American Psychologist* summarised the problems for the field. Zimbardo responded that the critiques didn't change the core lesson about situational power.

## So what's left?

Something real, but narrower than the legend. The Stanford study shows that:

- Institutions can make cruelty feel routine very fast, especially when the people in charge signal that it's wanted.
- Roles, uniforms and anonymity make it easier to act in ways you wouldn't as yourself.
- Most people present, including those who disapproved, did nothing to stop it.

What it does *not* show is that anyone put in a guard's uniform automatically becomes a tyrant. Which is exactly why we built [Block 9](/play) to separate the pressures, assigning anonymity, authority pressure and peer behaviour at random, and see which ones actually move people.
