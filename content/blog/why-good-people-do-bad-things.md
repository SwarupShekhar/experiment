Most people believe they would be one of the good ones: the guard who refuses, the employee who speaks up. Decades of research suggest we're worse at predicting our own behaviour than we think. Here are six forces that keep showing up.

## 1. Anonymity

When people feel unidentifiable, restraint drops. In a 1969 study, Zimbardo found women wearing hoods delivered longer "shocks" than those wearing name tags. A well-known 1976 field study of Halloween trick-or-treaters found children in groups, whose names were unknown, were more likely to take extra sweets when left alone. Psychologists call this **deindividuation**.

## 2. Authority

People will do things on instruction they would never do on their own. Milgram's obedience studies (see our [explainer](/blog/milgram-obedience-experiments-explained)) found most volunteers would deliver what they thought were dangerous shocks when a calm authority told them to continue.

## 3. Conformity

In Solomon Asch's line-judgement experiments in the 1950s, about three-quarters of participants went along with an obviously wrong group answer at least once. If people will deny what they can see to fit in, it's not surprising they'll go along with a colleague's "harmless" cruelty.

## 4. Dehumanising language

Calling people by numbers, slurs or animal names makes harming them easier. In a 1975 study by Albert Bandura, participants gave stronger punishments to people they had overheard being described as "animals". Labels are cheap and they work.

## 5. Gradual escalation

Nobody starts at 450 volts. Milgram's shocks rose in 15-volt steps; Stanford's routines got harsher each night. Each step is only slightly worse than the last one you already accepted, which makes it hard to find a place to stop. This is the **foot-in-the-door** dynamic.

## 6. Incentives and targets

When a system rewards a number (arrests, order, sales), people optimise for the number. The pattern is known as Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure, and people can hurt others while telling themselves they're just doing the job.

## And the counterweights

The same research shows what helps: **being identifiable**, **seeing the victim up close**, **one other person refusing** (in Milgram's variants, a single defiant peer made most people refuse), and **naming the thing out loud**, the way Christina Maslach did at Stanford.
