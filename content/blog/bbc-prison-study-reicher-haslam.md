Thirty years after Stanford, social psychologists **Alex Haslam** and **Steve Reicher** did something nobody had done: they ran the prison study again, properly controlled and with an independent ethics committee watching, and broadcast it on the BBC as *The Experiment* (2002).

## What was different

Fifteen men were split into guards and prisoners. Unlike Stanford, the researchers did **not** play the role of prison authorities or tell the guards what to do. They also built in planned interventions, such as telling prisoners that promotion to guard was possible, and then later closing that door, to test specific ideas about group identity.

## What happened

**The guards never really became a team.** Many were uncomfortable with authority, worried about being seen as bullies, and disagreed about how to use their power. They did not spontaneously invent humiliating routines.

**The prisoners did become a team.** Once promotion was ruled out, the prisoners developed a shared identity, organised, and challenged the guards. Within days the guard regime collapsed.

**Then things got darker.** The participants set up a self-governing commune. When that struggled, some participants began planning a hard-line takeover, with a new, stricter set of guards. The study was ended at that point.

## What Reicher and Haslam concluded

Tyranny, they argue, doesn't come from people slipping "naturally" into roles. It comes when people **identify with a group and its leadership**, and when a system seems to be failing, authoritarian solutions start to look attractive. Cruelty is less about losing yourself in a role and more about actively believing in what your group is doing.

This reading fits Stanford too: the guards there were given a leader (Zimbardo), a mission ("create a sense of powerlessness") and permission.
