Ten years before Stanford, Yale psychologist **Stanley Milgram** ran what is probably the most replicated disturbing finding in social psychology.

## The setup

Volunteers were told they were in a study about learning and punishment. They were the "teacher"; another person, actually an actor, was the "learner", strapped into a chair in the next room. Every time the learner got a word pair wrong, the teacher had to press a switch delivering an electric shock, starting at 15 volts and rising in 15-volt steps to 450 volts. The top switches were labelled "Danger: Severe Shock" and then simply "XXX".

No shocks were real. But as the voltage rose, the learner complained, then screamed, then begged to be let out, mentioned a heart condition, and finally fell silent.

When the teacher hesitated, the experimenter, calm in a lab coat, used a fixed sequence of prods, roughly: *please continue*; *the experiment requires that you continue*; *it is absolutely essential that you continue*; *you have no other choice, you must go on.*

## The result

In the best-known version (published 1963), **26 of 40 participants, 65%, went all the way to 450 volts.** Almost everyone went past the point where the learner was screaming. Many were visibly distressed: sweating, laughing nervously, arguing. They kept going anyway.

Before the study, Milgram asked psychiatrists to predict the outcome. They guessed about one in a thousand would go to the end.

## The variations are the real lesson

Milgram ran many versions, and obedience swung dramatically with the situation:

- When the learner was in the same room, obedience dropped. When the teacher had to physically press the learner's hand onto a plate, it dropped further.
- When the experimenter gave orders by phone, obedience fell sharply, and some participants quietly gave lower shocks than instructed.
- When two other "teachers" (actors) refused to continue, the vast majority of real participants refused too.

Milgram's explanation was the **agentic state**: once people see themselves as the instrument of someone else's authority, they stop feeling responsible for what they do.

## Criticism and replications

Later archive work by Gina Perry found the prods weren't always delivered as scripted, and that many participants doubted the shocks were real. Modern researchers (notably Haslam and Reicher) argue people weren't blindly obeying but actively *identifying* with the scientific goal, which is why the prod "you have no other choice" usually made people *stop*.

Even so, the core effect has held up. In 2009, Jerry Burger ran an ethical partial replication stopping at 150 volts, the point where the learner first demands to be released, and found about 70% of participants were willing to continue past it.
